# Chapter 6

The fittings came out wrong.

Not catastrophically — they'd hold, they'd seal, the commissioner would never know — but Cass knew, and Aldric knew, and when they laid them out on the cooling rack in a row, neither of them said anything for a long moment. The bronze had a slight irregularity in the upper flanges, a hitch in the curve that shouldn't have been there. His father's template, the wooden form that had been hanging on the same nail for eleven years, was fine. The pour had been clean. The problem was somewhere in the middle, in the translation between the form's intention and the metal's execution, and that kind of problem was the hardest kind.

"Cooling too fast?" Cass asked.

His father picked up the nearest fitting and turned it. His thumb found the bad curve without looking. "No."

"The alloy ratio—"

"Was correct." Aldric set it down. "I watched you measure."

Cass had measured correctly. He was sure of that, almost. He looked at the fitting. It looked back at him, or didn't — it was bronze, it didn't look at anything — but the flaw was there, a tiny deviation that his eye kept returning to like a tongue finding a loose tooth.

"I'll do them again," he said.

"After the meal." His father covered the rack with the inspection cloth. "It's past fourth bell."

The foundry in the evening had a different quality to it than the foundry at work. The fires were banked, the bellows still, the smell of hot metal cooling to something drier and less urgent. Cass had always liked it, the shift from the noise of making to the quiet of having made. He helped his father close the dampers and then stood in the middle of the floor while Aldric washed his hands at the basin, and the under-sound was there, steady, the way it had been all day. Present. Not worse. Not better. He'd stopped expecting it to do anything and it had settled into something he carried rather than something that happened to him, which he wasn't sure was an improvement.

Maret was gone. He'd looked when he came back to the floor and her chair was empty, the book she'd been reading left face-down on the arm of it. He didn't know when she'd left or where she'd gone, and he'd spent the rest of the afternoon telling himself this was fine, which it was, because she was a stranger and her presence or absence had nothing to do with him.

The book was still there. He glanced at it now. Some kind of technical manual, he thought, though from here he couldn't read the spine.

"Come up," his father said. "Your sister made the bread."

The bread was good. Lenne had a gift for it, the same gift she had for most things involving patience and precision and a willingness to do the same step three times until it was right. She was fourteen and she moved through the kitchen with a confidence Cass had never managed in any room, setting the table around her father's elbows with the efficiency of someone who had long ago stopped waiting for anyone to move.

"There's a letter," she said, without looking up from the soup.

"From who?" Cass asked.

"Whom." She pointed at the sideboard. "It came this afternoon. I don't know. I didn't open it."

He picked it up. The seal was unfamiliar, a plain circle pressed in brown wax, no device. His name on the front in a hand he didn't recognize, careful and small. He turned it over. Nothing on the back.

"Are you going to open it?" Lenne asked.

"In a minute."

"You've been holding it for a minute."

He broke the seal. Inside, a single sheet, folded twice. He unfolded it and read it and then read it again, and the under-sound did what it did, steady, and the needle didn't come but something adjacent to it did, a pressure at the back of his jaw like a tooth that hadn't decided yet whether it was going to hurt.

The letter was from the Bellwright's Hall. Not from the Guildmaster — the hand was too careful, too small — but from someone there, someone who signed with a title he'd seen on official correspondence before but never had occasion to think about. *Assessor of the Resonant Arts.* He'd never heard of anyone holding that title. He wasn't sure it had been held in his lifetime.

The letter said, in careful and very small handwriting, that his presence was requested at the Hall at the second bell of the morning, three days hence. It said that this was not in connection with any irregularity or violation. It said that the matter concerned his recent work and that he would be compensated for his time.

It did not say what the matter was.

"Well?" Lenne said.

"It's from the Hall." He put the letter on the sideboard. "They want to see me."

His father looked up from his bread. His face did the thing it sometimes did, a very slight stilling, like a pool when the wind stops. "What for?"

"They didn't say."

A pause. His father tore his bread and ate it. "The Guildmaster?"

"An Assessor. I don't know who."

"Assessor of what?"

Cass picked the letter up again and read him the title. His father's expression didn't change, exactly, but something shifted behind it. He looked down at his soup.

"You know what that is," Cass said.

"I know what it used to be." Aldric picked up his spoon. "Eat your meal."

Lenne looked at Cass. Cass looked at Lenne. She made a very small gesture with her chin, *later*, and he sat down and ate his soup, which was good, and the bread was good, and the under-sound sat at the base of his skull and kept its own counsel.

---

Later meant after their father had gone back to the foundry to check the dampers a second time, the way he always did, a habit from the early years when he'd been afraid of fire in the night. Cass helped Lenne clear the table and she didn't say anything until the bowls were stacked and the cloth was folded.

"He recognized it," she said. "The title."

"I know."

"He's not going to tell you."

"I know."

She sat down at the table, which meant she wanted to talk properly, so he sat across from her. She had their mother's forehead, wide and very still, and she thought before she spoke in a way that sometimes made Cass feel careless by comparison. She was looking at the letter, which he'd brought to the table without deciding to.

"There was a girl," she said, "at the Alderman's festival last autumn. She was with the delegation from the eastern guilds. You weren't there."

"I was at the Pell commission."

"I know. She was — she did something. During the ceremonial ringing, she stood too close to the great bell and when it struck she—" Lenne paused. "She didn't fall. But she went very still, and afterward she was sick, and the Hall men who were with the delegation took her somewhere and I never saw her again at the festival."

Cass waited.

"I asked Mama Vetch about it later," Lenne said. Mama Vetch was the woman two streets over who had been friends with their mother and who knew approximately everything about approximately everyone in the city. "She said the girl was a resonant. That's the word she used."

"I've heard that word," Cass said.

"She said it happens sometimes. Not often. Someone who can hear the pitch of things, the real pitch, not just the sound. She said the Hall keeps track of them." Lenne looked at him steadily. "She said they have someone whose job it is."

The pressure at the back of his jaw again. He put his hand flat on the table.

"Cass." Lenne's voice was careful. "The fittings today."

"Were a bad pour."

"Were they?"

He opened his mouth and closed it. She waited, patient, the way she was patient with bread dough, giving it time to do what it was going to do.

"I don't know," he said, which was the truest thing he'd said all day.

Lenne nodded, as if he'd confirmed something. She picked up the letter and read it herself, the whole thing, slowly. Then she set it back down.

"Three days," she said.

"Yes."

"That's not very long to worry about it."

"I wasn't planning to worry about it."

She looked at him, and the look said she knew him better than that, and he looked back, and the look said he knew she was right but wasn't going to say so. They'd had this exchange, in various forms, for most of their lives.

"The woman," he said. "The one from before. Maret."

Lenne's expression shifted slightly. "The one Father let in this morning."

"She explained something to me." He picked his words. "About the — about what's been happening. She said it means something is trying to happen that isn't quite happening yet." He heard how that sounded. "She put it differently. Better."

"She sounds like she knows what she's talking about."

"She might. I don't know who she is."

"Did you ask?"

He hadn't. He'd sat across from her in the chair room for half an hour and asked her about the pain and about bells and had not thought to ask her name beyond what she'd given, which was just the name, no house, no guild affiliation, nothing. "Not really," he said.

Lenne folded her hands on the table. "You should find her before you go to the Hall."

"Why?"

"Because whatever they want to assess," she said, "it would be better to know something before you walk in there. Even a little something." She paused. "And because she came here, which means she knows something about you, or about this, and you don't know anything about her, and that's a bad position to be in."

He thought about Maret's face when she'd said *don't get used to it*. The way the almost-smile had landed, there and gone.

"She'll probably come back," he said.

"Probably isn't a plan."

"Lenne."

"I'm just saying."

He took the letter and folded it along its original creases and put it in his pocket. The under-sound was there, the same as always, and he sat with his sister at the cleared table for another minute, not talking, which was one of the things they were good at together.

"The fittings," he said finally. "I'm going to redo them tomorrow."

"I know," she said.

"I measured correctly."

She looked at him for a moment. Then: "Did you hear anything, while you were doing them?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it.

"The under-sound," he said. "It was there. It's always there now."

"Is it louder when you're working?"

He thought about the pour, the moment of tipping the ladle, the way the bronze had moved. He'd been focused on the measurement, on the temperature, on the form. The sound had been in the background, the way it always was, but — there had been a moment. When he'd tilted the ladle and the metal had started to move, there had been a moment when the sound had shifted, not louder but *different*, a change in quality he hadn't paid attention to because he'd been watching the pour.

"Maybe," he said.

Lenne said nothing. She got up and took the lamp from the sideboard and carried it to the window, and for a moment she stood there with the light behind her, looking out at the dark street, and Cass looked at the back of her head and thought about the fitting's bad curve, about the moment of the pour, about the sound that had shifted and that he'd ignored.

"Go to bed," she said, without turning around. "You have fittings to redo tomorrow."

He went. On the stairs he passed his father coming up from the foundry, and they nodded at each other, and his father paused for just a moment on the step below him and looked at him in the dim stairwell with that still-pool expression, and then looked away and went on up. Neither of them said anything.

His room was small, the same room he'd had since childhood, with the same shelf his father had built and the same window that looked out over the alley between their building and the tanner's. He sat on the edge of his bed and took the letter out of his pocket and read it one more time by the light of the candle on his bedside table.

*Assessor of the Resonant Arts.*

He'd been at the Hall a hundred times. For inspections, for commission registrations, for the apprentice assessments that were part of the guild's ordinary business. He knew the building, the smell of it, old stone and beeswax and the particular dusty-metal smell of a place where bells had been stored and moved and catalogued for three hundred years. He knew the front rooms, the registration hall, the inspection gallery. He'd never been to the upper floors, where the administrative offices were, where the Guildmaster's chambers were, where the records went back to the founding of the guild and further.

He'd never had reason to go.

The candle guttered in a draft from the window. He didn't move to close it.

Three days was not very long. Lenne was right about that, and she was right about Maret, and she was probably right about most things, which was a fact Cass had learned to accept without resentment sometime around his twelfth year. It didn't mean he knew what to do. Find Maret, she'd said, as if Maret were findable, as if he had any way to locate a woman who'd appeared in his father's foundry and then vanished.

Though she'd found him.

Whatever she was, whatever she knew, she'd found him. Which meant she had a reason to be found, or she didn't, or she was waiting for him to make a move, or she wasn't, and he was sitting in his room at night spinning possibilities the way bad apprentices spun excuses, round and round and getting nowhere.

He put the letter on the shelf. Lay down. The under-sound was there, the same as always, and the needle was quiet, and outside the tanner's dog barked once at something and then stopped.

He slept, eventually.

In the morning the fittings were still wrong. He looked at them before breakfast, pulling the cloth back to study them in the early light that came through the foundry's high windows, and the flaw was still there, that hitch in the upper flange, subtle enough to miss if you weren't looking and impossible to ignore once you'd seen it. He put the cloth back.

His father was at the bench, doing something with a small file, not the fittings. Not looking at Cass.

"I'm going to walk before I start," Cass said.

His father didn't look up. "Don't be long."

The street was cold, the kind of cold that came in from the river in the mornings before the city fully woke, and Cass walked without a particular direction, past the bread-sellers and the early carters, past the chandler's shop with its sweet heavy smell, past the corner where a man was selling hot chestnuts from a brazier, the smoke going straight up in the still air. The under-sound walked with him, as it had been doing, a constant low companion.

He turned toward the Hall without deciding to.

He wasn't going there, not yet, not for three days. He knew that. But he walked in that direction, through the old part of the district where the streets were narrower and the buildings leaned toward each other, and he came out onto the square where the Hall stood, and he stood on the far side of the square and looked at it.

It was an old building. Not the oldest in the city, but old enough, three stories of gray stone with the guild device carved above the entrance, two bells crossed over a pair of calipers. The windows on the upper floors were dark. The square was mostly empty at this hour, a few pigeons, a woman with a market basket cutting across the far corner.

He looked at the upper windows.

And then he heard, very clearly, a voice behind him.

"You shouldn't have come here today."

He turned. Maret was standing at the edge of the square, a few feet away, her coat different from yesterday's but the same quality of worn-in, the same expression that wasn't quite watchful and wasn't quite calm.

"How did you—" He stopped. "You followed me."

"You walked to the Hall the morning after receiving the Hall's letter," she said. "I didn't have to follow you anywhere. I just had to be here."

"You knew about the letter."

She looked at him for a moment. Then: "Come away from the square. There are windows."

He wanted to argue, but she was already moving, back into the narrow street he'd come from, and he followed her because the alternative was standing in the square alone looking at dark windows, which seemed worse. She led him half a block and stopped in the doorway of a closed shop, out of the sight-line to the Hall.

"The letter is from a man named Orvast," she said. "He's been the Assessor for about four years. Before that there wasn't one, because there was no one to assess. Now there is."

"Me."

"Not only you." She looked at him directly. "There are three others in the city. You're the most recent."

He put his back against the doorframe and looked at her. She was, he thought, maybe thirty, or maybe older in a way that didn't show the same as age usually did. Her hands were in her coat pockets. She looked like someone waiting for a cart, except for the way she was watching him.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"I told you my name."

"That's not what I asked."

She was quiet for a moment, which wasn't the same as hesitating. More like someone deciding how much of a true thing to say. "I was assessed," she said, "eleven years ago. By the man who had the position before Orvast. I know what the meeting is. I know what they'll ask you to do and what they'll offer you for it. And I know what they won't tell you." She paused. "I came to your father's foundry because I wanted to see the thing before they got to it."

"The thing," he said. "You mean me."

"I mean what's happening in you." She said it without apology, but also without cruelty. "It's not often that it comes on this way, through craft work. Usually it's a sudden exposure, a large bell, a resonant event. Yours is growing in slowly. That's rarer. Orvast will find it interesting."

Cass looked at the Hall's square, or the edge of it, what he could see from the doorway. The crossed bells above the entrance, too far to make out now. "What will they ask me to do?"

"Test bells," she said. "Find flaws that don't show in the metal. Detect pitch problems before a bell is hung. There's a guild function for it, a very old one, that's been empty for a long time." She watched him. "It's not a bad life. If that's what you want."

"And if it's not."

She didn't answer right away. The under-sound was there between them, or in him, and he had the odd thought that she could hear it too, that she was listening to something in the air that he was only beginning to learn was audible.

"Then you should know that before you walk in," she said. "That's all."

He looked at her. She looked back. A cart went past the end of the street, wheels loud on the stone, and the sound of it moved through him and the under-sound shifted for a moment, that same quality-change he'd noticed during the pour, a response to something external, an answering.

The needle came, sharp, behind his left eye.

He pressed two fingers to his temple, a reflex.

Maret watched him do it. Her expression didn't change, but she reached into her coat pocket and brought out something small, a piece of folded paper, and held it out. He took it.

"That's where I'm staying," she said. "Come before you go to the Hall. Not the morning of — the day before."

He looked at the paper. An address, a street name, a number.

"Why are you doing this?" he asked.

She put her hands back in her pockets. Looked at the end of the street where the cart had gone.

"Because no one did it for me," she said, simply, and then she walked away, back toward the square, and he stood in the doorway with the paper in his hand and the needle fading and the under-sound steady as a held note, and watched her go.
