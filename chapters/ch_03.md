# Chapter Three

The east annex was not on any map Cass had seen, which meant either it predated the maps or it had been left off on purpose. He suspected the latter. The corridor leading to it ran behind the main archive, low-ceilinged and smelling of old lime wash and something else beneath that, something mineral and faintly sweet, the way the air smelled near the casting pit when they were running silver alloy. He followed Edric's lamp.

"How long has she been here?" Cass asked.

"Since before I came."

"Who brought her?"

Edric's boots scuffed on the stone. "I don't know who brought her. She was here. She's always been here, as far as anyone I've asked can tell." He paused at a door, heavy oak banded in iron gone brown with age, and took a key from his belt. "I want you to understand something before we go in."

Cass waited.

"She will hear things in you. Things you haven't told anyone. That's not a threat, it's just — she does that. Try not to be frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

Edric looked at him in the lamp-light, and the look was not unkind, but it was not agreement either. He turned the key.

---

The room was smaller than Cass expected. He'd built it up overnight into something vaulted and resonant, a space that matched the strangeness of what Edric had told him, but it was just a room: stone floor, single window slit high in the wall, a shelf of books, a stool, and in the corner a chair with a woman in it. She was old. Not fragile-old, not hunched and diminished, but the kind of old that had stopped being a process and become a state, like bronze that had finished patinating and settled into its final color. Her hands on the chair arms were large, knuckled, the hands of someone who had worked with them.

She was looking at him before he'd fully come through the door.

"Cass," she said. Not a question. Her voice was lower than he'd expected, a working voice, the kind that had given orders.

"Yes, ma'am."

A small movement at the corner of her mouth. "Sit down. Edric, you can go."

Edric set the lamp on the shelf. He glanced at Cass once, something in it that Cass could not quite read, and then he went out and pulled the door behind him. The latch clicked.

Cass found the stool. The woman watched him carry it over and set it down in front of her chair, and she didn't help or hinder, just watched.

"You heard it yesterday," she said.

"In the bell."

"You've been hearing it longer than that."

He opened his mouth and closed it again. The lamp flame was steady. No draft in here, the walls thick enough to swallow everything outside.

"How long?" she asked.

"I don't — a few months. Maybe more. I thought it was tinnitus. My father has it, from the foundry. I thought it was that."

"But you didn't really think it was that."

He looked at his hands. Burn scar on the right thumb, ink stain on the left forefinger from the ledger work. "No."

She nodded, the way someone nods when they've been told something they already knew but needed to hear said aloud. "What does it sound like?"

"It's — " He stopped. Tried to find words that weren't stupid. "It's below everything. Below the room tone. Like if you took the lowest note you could sing and went down from there until it stopped being sound and started being something else. Except it's always sound. It's always exactly at the edge of being sound."

"Does it hurt?"

"When I pay attention to it. When something makes it louder." He pressed two fingers against his left eye socket, an unconscious gesture he caught himself doing. "Behind here. Like a — like a needle going in."

"A needle," she repeated. She seemed to be tasting the word. "Not a pressure."

"No. Specific. Like it's pointing at something."

She was quiet for a moment. Outside, somewhere far enough away to be almost nothing, a bell rang. Not the refectory bell, something smaller, a handbell, two notes, and Cass heard the under-sound shift when the second note faded, a tiny adjustment, like a tuning fork correcting itself.

The needle moved. He kept his face still.

"You felt that," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She sat forward slightly, hands still on the chair arms. "I'm going to tell you some things, and I want you to listen without deciding whether to believe them until I've finished. Can you do that?"

"I can try."

She looked at him. "That's a better answer than most people give me."

Her name, she told him, was Maret. She had no title that she used. She had been at the House of Bells for thirty-one years, brought by the master before Edric's master, brought because she could hear the deep note and the master had needed someone who could.

"Needed for what?" Cass asked, and she held up one finger, and he subsided.

The House of Bells cast bells. He knew that. He knew the commissions, the churches, the great towers in the city, the smaller bells for ships and schools. He knew the alloys and the molds and the tuning work. But the House also cast bells for a different purpose, had always done so, and those bells did not go to churches.

"What do they do?" He couldn't help it.

Maret looked at him without irritation. "They hold things. The way a vessel holds water. The bell is the vessel. The deep note is what you're holding." She paused. "The word for it in the old books is *anker*. An anchor. Something that keeps a thing in one place rather than letting it drift."

"What things?"

"Presences," she said. "Not ghosts. Not demons. The books are full of people trying to name them and getting it wrong. Think of them as — resonances. Places where the world has been struck hard enough that it keeps vibrating. A battle. A fire. A place where someone died badly and the dying didn't quite finish." Her hands moved on the chair arms, a small restless movement. "They're not malevolent. Most of them. They're just unfinished."

Cass thought about the under-sound, the way it had been there all summer like a note held past its natural end. "And the bells—"

"The bells give them a fixed point. Something to resolve into. The vibration finds the bell and stops drifting." She looked at him steadily. "That's the simple version."

"What's the complicated version?"

"The complicated version takes years." But she said it without dismissal. More like: *you'll get there.* "What I need to know right now is whether you can hear the note clearly enough to tune for it."

"Tune a bell to the under-sound?"

"Yes."

He thought about this. He thought about the bell he'd finished yesterday, the way the note had felt right before he'd struck it, the alignment of shoulder and waist, the tiny adjustments he'd made to the inner curve. He thought about how the under-sound had responded when the bell rang, not matching it exactly but — relating to it. The way two strings an octave apart related.

"I don't know," he said. "I've never tried."

"That's honest." She settled back in her chair. "Edric thinks you're ready to try. He's been watching you for two years."

"He hasn't said anything."

"No. He wouldn't." She looked at him with something that might have been amusement, might have been something older than that. "He didn't say anything to me either, when I found him. He was seventeen and he'd been having headaches for a year and he'd broken three mallets trying to tune a bell he could hear was wrong in a way he couldn't explain."

Cass looked up.

"He hears it too?"

"Heard it. He doesn't anymore. That happens, sometimes. The ability fades." She said it without particular sorrow, just as a fact, the way you'd say a seam had shifted in casting. "He can still work with the bells. He knows the theory. But he can't hear the note itself, hasn't been able to for eight years. Which is part of why he came to me when you started showing the signs."

The signs. Cass turned that over. "What are the signs?"

"Headaches behind the left eye. Difficulty sleeping in rooms with bells present. Occasionally — and this is the one that usually frightens people — occasionally hearing conversations that aren't happening. Not voices exactly. More like the shape of speech. The feeling that something nearby is trying to use language and not quite managing it."

The refectory, three weeks ago. He'd been sitting with Pell and the other apprentices and he'd heard — he'd thought he'd heard — someone talking under the table noise, something that kept almost resolving into words. He'd looked around. Nobody.

He'd told himself it was the acoustics. The refectory had odd acoustics.

"The refectory," he said.

Maret nodded. "There was a fire there. Hundred and forty years ago. Before the building was the refectory. Three people died."

"And the bell holds them?"

"There's a bell in the kitchen passage. Small one, iron, very old. It's been there a long time. It's not enough anymore — iron degrades, the tuning shifts. The presence has been getting louder." She paused. "You've been hearing it for three weeks."

He sat with that. The lamp flame moved, some tiny shift in the air, and steadied again.

"What happens if the bell fails completely?"

"Nothing dramatic. The presence doesn't become dangerous. It just becomes — ambient. Pervasive. The people in the refectory start having bad dreams. Fights over small things. Someone cuts their hand on a knife that slipped wrong." She tilted her head. "You've noticed the mood in there lately."

He had. He'd thought it was the autumn, the long hours, the cold coming in. Pell had been short-tempered for weeks. Two of the older apprentices had nearly come to blows over a stool.

"We need a new bell for the kitchen passage," Maret said. "Properly tuned. That's what you'll be working on."

"With Edric?"

"Edric will oversee the casting. The tuning is yours." She looked at him without particular drama. "You're the only one here who can hear what it needs to hear."

He should have felt the weight of that. He could tell it was meant to be weighty. Instead he felt something more like what he'd felt yesterday, holding the finished bell before he struck it — the particular stillness of a thing about to become what it was supposed to be.

"When do I start?" he asked.

"You already have." She stood, which he hadn't expected. She was taller than he'd thought, her movement deliberate but not slow. She crossed to the shelf and took down a book, small, brown-covered, the spine cracked and re-glued badly. She held it out to him. "Take this. Don't lend it. Don't leave it where other apprentices will find it and ask questions."

He took it. The cover had no title. The leather was warm from being near the lamp.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You've been asking me things for twenty minutes."

"Something different." He held the book in both hands. "The under-sound. The deep note. Is it — is it one thing? Is it everywhere at once? Or is it—"

"That," Maret said, "is the right question." She sat back down. For a moment she looked at the window slit, the thin grey light coming through it. "What do you think?"

He thought about the way it had shifted when the handbell rang outside. The small adjustment, like a correction. He thought about how it was louder in some rooms and quieter in others, louder near the old iron bell in the kitchen passage, louder in the courtyard near the big casting furnace. He thought about how it had been there, constant, all summer, but with variations, a texture to it, not one smooth tone but something with — motion in it. Like water that looked still but had a current.

"I think it's one thing," he said slowly. "But it's not the same everywhere. Like — like a bell has one note but the note sounds different depending on where you're standing. The bell doesn't change. The relationship changes."

Maret looked at him for a long moment.

"Go," she said. "Read the first section. Come back in three days."

He stood and picked up the stool and returned it to where it had been, by the wall. He didn't know why he did that. It seemed like the right thing.

At the door he stopped. "What do I tell Edric?"

"Nothing. He'll know how it went."

He nodded and went out into the low corridor, pulling the door shut behind him. The latch clicked. The lamp he'd brought was still on the shelf inside, and the corridor was dark, but there was enough light from the archive end to walk by, and he'd been in darker places.

He walked back through the corridor with the book under his arm. The mineral smell faded behind him, and then the lime wash, and then he was in the archive passage with its familiar smells of dust and tallow and old paper, and ahead of him the sounds of the foundry floor, hammers and fire and the human noise of work.

The under-sound was there. It was always there. But he was listening to it differently now, the way you listened differently to a bell once you knew what it was made of.

He stopped walking.

The book under his arm. The needle behind his left eye, gone quiet for now, resting. The under-sound doing what it always did, holding its position just below the threshold of what the ear wanted to call sound.

He'd asked Maret if it was one thing. She'd said it was the right question. She hadn't said it was the right answer.

He filed that away and went to find Edric.
