# Chapter Five

The catalogue was finished by the time the foundry floor went quiet.

Cass set the pen down and listened to the quiet, which was different from silence — the furnace still breathed, the metal still ticked and settled as it cooled, and underneath all of it the under-sound did what it always did. He had stopped noticing it again somewhere around commission forty-seven. Now he noticed it again. He wasn't sure if that was progress.

He stacked the records and left them on the worktable and carried the lamp down the hall to the kitchen, where he found half a loaf of bread going stiff at the cut end and a wedge of hard cheese and ate standing over the washbasin without lighting a second lamp. The bread was fine if you didn't think too much about the texture. He ate all of it.

Upstairs, in the room that had been his brother's and was now his, he lay on top of the blanket and looked at the ceiling and let himself think about what Edric had said.

*She gave me a different book.*

Which meant Maret had heard "many things" and understood it as an answer that wanted a different question. Which meant she had a way of listening that caught what you actually said instead of what you meant to say. Or maybe she caught both, and used whichever one was sharper.

Cass turned onto his side. The ceiling wasn't going anywhere.

He slept.

---

Morning came in gray, the kind that couldn't decide between cloud and fog, and Cass was on the foundry floor before his father, which had never happened before. He stood in the middle of it with the furnace not yet lit and the moulds in their rows along the east wall and the big sand-casting table bare and raked smooth, and he listened.

The under-sound was very clear in the cold.

He walked to the east wall. The sound was the same. He walked to the north, where the raw metal came in, and it was the same. West, where the finished work went out on its hooks to be inspected, and it was the same. He stood in the center again and closed his eyes.

It wasn't directional. He'd half expected it to be — expected to feel it pulling toward something the way the sound from a cracked bell pulled toward the crack if you pressed your ear close. But this came from everywhere or nowhere, which meant either the source was very large or it was very close. Inside the building, maybe. Inside the walls.

Inside him, maybe. He'd thought that before and not liked the thought and he still didn't like it but he held it for a moment before setting it aside.

He heard his father's step on the stairs and opened his eyes.

Edric came through the door with two cups, handed one to Cass without comment, and went to the furnace. Cass watched him open the damper and check the fuel and go through the sequence he'd gone through every morning for twenty years, and it occurred to him that his father never varied the order. Damper, fuel, tinder, bellows, then standing back to watch the catch. Always in that order. He'd thought it was habit but maybe it was something else — maybe the order mattered the way order mattered in bronze work, where you couldn't add the tin after the copper had set and expect anything good.

The fire caught. Edric stood back.

"You were down here early," Edric said.

"I wanted to try something."

"And?"

"It's not directional. The sound. I thought it might point somewhere but it doesn't."

Edric turned from the furnace. He had the same expression he used when Cass made a reasonable observation about something he'd already considered. Not dismissive. Just — ahead of it. "No," he said. "It doesn't."

Cass held his cup in both hands. The warmth was coming through the clay. "You knew that."

"I suspected it. There's a difference." Edric picked up the long-handled poker and adjusted something in the furnace that didn't appear to need adjusting. "Did you bring the records from yesterday?"

"They're on the worktable."

"Good." He set the poker back. "We have a commission today. Decorative work, gate fittings for the Verath house. You'll do the pour."

Cass had done pours before, supervised, but not alone. "All of it?"

"I'll be here."

Which meant no, not alone, but also yes, alone. That was the distinction Edric made, and Cass had learned not to ask for clarification because the clarification was always doing the thing.

He did the pour. It went well enough — the metal moved cleanly into the mould and he held his breath during the last of it the way you always did, waiting for the bubble or the split, and neither came. Edric watched from six feet back and said nothing until it was done, and then said "good" and went back to his own work.

The morning passed that way, in the particular rhythm of foundry work, which was long stretches of heat and waiting broken by short stretches of precision. Cass liked it. He'd always liked it without knowing that he liked it, the same way you didn't know you liked a particular smell until you were somewhere it wasn't.

He was cleaning the mould when the bell above the street door rang.

---

He heard her voice before he saw her — low and specific, asking the apprentice Tomis something about the gate fittings, whether they were complete. Tomis said they weren't, not until afternoon, and Maret said she'd wait, and then she came through the inner door into the foundry and stopped when she saw Cass.

"Ah," she said. "You're here."

"I work here."

"Yes, I know." She looked around the foundry floor with the look of someone who had been in many foundries and was cataloguing this one. She was wearing the same coat as before, or one very like it. "Where's your father?"

"Back room. I can get him."

"In a moment." She walked to the east wall and looked at the moulds. She picked one up, the small one for the hinge plates, and turned it over. "You did the pour this morning?"

"How did you know that?"

"Your father's pours are slightly more aggressive. He pushes the metal. Yours is more careful." She set the mould down. "Not worse. Different."

Cass put down the cloth he'd been using on the mould. "Is that why you're here? To look at the pour?"

"I'm here for the gate fittings." She turned from the wall. "But since you're here — did you try the under-sound? On the floor?"

He looked at her. She was watching him with the same quality of attention she'd had in her workroom, the kind that didn't fill in the gaps with what it expected to hear. He'd met maybe three people in his life who listened that way.

"It's not directional," he said.

"No."

"You knew."

"I've been listening to it for thirty years." She pulled a stool from under the worktable and sat on it without asking, which was something Edric also did. "What else?"

Cass picked up the cloth again, turned it in his hands. "It's clearer in the cold. This morning before the furnace was lit, it was — it was very clear. Like when you clean a bell and the tone comes up."

Maret looked at him steadily. "Yes," she said. "That's right."

"What does that mean?"

"It means what you said. It's clearer when the other sounds are quieter." She paused. "What did you think it meant?"

"I don't know. I thought maybe it meant something about the source. That the source was — temperature-sensitive, or —" He stopped. He was reaching for a framework that didn't fit. He could feel it the way you felt a word that wasn't quite right, the slight wrongness of it. "I don't know."

"That's a better answer than the other one."

He looked up.

"The one you were about to give me," she said. "The one you made up because you thought I wanted a complete thought. 'I don't know' is a complete thought."

Cass set the cloth down. "Edric said you gave him a different book. When he told you it was many things."

Maret's expression shifted — not much, just enough that something in it became less neutral. "He told you that."

"Last night. He said he'd heard it as many things and you gave him a different book."

"I remember." She was quiet for a moment. The furnace did what furnaces did, the low constant push of heat and breath. "What do you hear it as?"

"One thing," Cass said. "Just one thing. But I don't know what thing."

Maret looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something unexpected: she laughed. Not long, not loud, just a short sound that seemed to surprise her as much as it surprised him. "Your father heard many things," she said, "because your father is very good at many things. He hears the world in its parts." She stood from the stool. "You might be different."

"Different how?"

"I don't know yet." She said it plainly, without apology. "That's an honest answer. I don't know yet."

The inner door opened and Edric came through, saw Maret, and stopped with the particular stillness of a man who had expected to walk into a room alone. His eyes moved to Cass and back to Maret.

"The fittings aren't ready," he said.

"I know. Tomis told me." She didn't seem bothered by this. "We were talking."

Edric looked at Cass again. Cass couldn't read the look exactly — it had something in it he didn't have a name for, not quite concern, not quite the thing before concern. "About what?"

"The under-sound," Cass said.

A pause. Something moved through Edric's face and was gone. "I see."

"I was telling him I don't know what he is yet," Maret said, and there was something in how she said it — matter-of-fact, like a casting note — that made it land harder than if she'd been careful with it. "I thought he should know that I don't know, rather than have him think I do."

Edric crossed to the furnace and stood with his back to both of them. He adjusted the damper, which didn't need adjusting. "The fittings will be ready by the fourth bell," he said. "You can wait in the front room."

"I'll wait here, if you don't mind."

"I do mind."

Maret looked at his back for a moment. Then she looked at Cass, and her expression said something he couldn't quite read, something between *you see* and *this is always how it is*, and then she picked up her coat from where she'd laid it on the worktable and went through the inner door.

Cass waited.

Edric didn't turn around.

"She shouldn't have said that to you," Edric said finally.

"Why not?"

"Because you're fourteen and you don't —" He stopped. Started again. "It's not the right time for that kind of — she moves too fast. She always moved too fast."

Cass looked at his father's back. The set of the shoulders. The hands on the furnace frame, not gripping, just resting, but resting with more intention than resting usually had. "She said she doesn't know what I am yet."

"I heard her."

"That doesn't seem like moving too fast. That seems like — the opposite."

Edric turned around. His face had the closed quality it got when he was working through something he hadn't finished working through. "She told me, when I was your age, that I had a particular gift. She was specific about it. She was certain." A pause. "She was right. But knowing it at fifteen didn't help me become it any faster. It just meant I spent three years trying to be what she'd said instead of finding out what I actually was."

Cass thought about this. "But she didn't say that to me."

"No."

"She said she didn't know."

"Yes." Edric looked at the floor, then up. "Yes, she did."

Something was sitting in the space between them, some understanding that was still becoming itself, and Cass had learned from the under-sound that you didn't push at that kind of thing. You waited.

"Finish the fittings," Edric said. "I'll be in the back."

He went.

Cass stood alone in the foundry and listened to the furnace and the under-sound and the small settling sounds of the building around him. He picked up the cloth. He went back to the mould.

He worked carefully, the way he'd poured carefully — not timid, just precise, giving each movement its right amount and no more. His father pushed the metal. Maret had said it like an observation, not a judgment, but it had landed somewhere. He turned it over while his hands worked.

His father had been told what he was at fifteen and had spent three years becoming it instead of finding it. That was a thing Edric had never said before. That was, possibly, the most direct thing Edric had ever said to him, and it had come wrapped in a correction of someone else, which was how Edric did his most direct things. Cass recognized the pattern now that he was looking at it.

The mould was clean. He set it in its place on the rack.

He went to the front room, where Maret was sitting in the chair by the window with a small book open on her knee, not reading it, looking at the street. She didn't look up when he came in.

"Can I ask you something?" Cass said.

"You can ask."

He sat on the bench across from her. "When you gave Edric a different book. What was the different book?"

Maret looked at him then. "A history of the Aldric foundry. Three generations of bell-casters in Meveth, all working from the same methods, all producing good work. The last of them made something — a cathedral bell, very large — that was different from everything before it. Not in the method. The method was the same. But in the result." She closed the book on her knee. "I wanted him to understand that the method and the gift are separate things."

"And the book I should read?"

"I don't know yet," she said again, and this time it didn't surprise him.

"But you have one in mind."

She looked at him for a moment. "Maybe."

"What is it?"

"It's not a book." She turned back to the window. "It's a place. But you're not ready for it yet and I'm not ready to take you, so that's where we are."

Cass sat with that. The street outside was doing its midday things — a cart going past, two women with baskets, a dog that had opinions about the cart. Normal street. Normal day. Maret watching it with the same quality of attention she gave everything, which made the street look slightly different, like a bell that had been cleaned.

"The under-sound," he said. "Do you hear it right now?"

"Yes."

"Does it ever —" He stopped, trying to find the shape of the question. "Does it ever hurt?"

Maret turned from the window slowly. Her face had changed in a way he couldn't name — not alarmed, but sharpened. "Where?"

"Behind my left eye. It's not always. Just sometimes, when it gets — I don't know. Louder isn't the right word. More present."

She was looking at him with an attention that was different from her usual attention, more focused, the way you focused on a pour when something in the metal's movement looked slightly wrong. "How long has that been happening?"

"A few weeks. Maybe a month."

"You didn't tell your father."

"No."

"Why not?"

Cass thought about this honestly. "Because I didn't know if it was real. I didn't know if I was making it up."

Maret set the book on the windowsill. "You're not making it up," she said. "And you should have told your father." She said it without heat, just as a fact to be registered. "The headache — is it happening now?"

He checked. The under-sound was there, steady, doing nothing unusual. "No."

"When it happens again, I want you to note what else is different. Whether the sound is actually louder or just — as you said — more present. Whether anything in the room has changed. Whether you've been working for a long time or just started." She picked up the book again and opened it, which seemed like the end of the conversation. Then she said, without looking up: "It's not a bad sign. The pain. In case you were wondering."

He hadn't been wondering, because he'd been trying not to, which was different. "What is it, then?"

"It means something is trying to happen that isn't quite happening yet." She turned a page. "Like a bell that hasn't been struck finding its pitch anyway, because something nearby is vibrating at the right frequency."

He looked at her. She was reading, or appearing to read.

"That's —" He stopped.

"What?"

"That's the first time you've actually explained something."

Maret looked up from the book. For a moment her expression was very still, and then the corner of her mouth moved in a way that wasn't quite a smile but was in the same family. "Don't get used to it," she said.

The clock on the wall — a plain thing, iron, one of his father's early pieces — read half past the third bell. The fittings would be ready in less than an hour. He should go back to the floor and help with the final cooling and inspection. He knew that.

He sat for another minute anyway, in the chair across from Maret, while she read and the under-sound did what it always did and the street outside went on being a street, and he let himself sit in the middle of not knowing what he was — not pushing at it, not trying to look at it directly, just letting it be there in the low light, taking whatever shape it was going to take.

The needle behind his left eye came and went in a single pulse, there and gone, like a string plucked once.

He noted it. He noted that nothing in the room had changed, that the sound was neither louder nor quieter, that he'd been sitting still for several minutes and hadn't been working hard. He noted that Maret turned a page at exactly that moment, though she couldn't have known, and that the page-turn made a small sound, and that the small sound sat in the air for just a breath before the under-sound absorbed it.

He got up and went back to the foundry floor.
