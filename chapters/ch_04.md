# Chapter Four

Edric was not in his office.

Cass checked the small room off the archive passage first, because that was where Edric usually sat in the late afternoon, going over the week's casting records with a candle and a cup of something that smelled like it had been boiled twice. The candle was there. The cup was there, still warm when Cass touched the side of it. But Edric wasn't.

He found him eventually on the foundry floor, standing at the edge of the casting pit with his arms crossed and his back to the door. The furnace was running hot — Cass could feel it from ten feet away, a wall of heat that pressed against the skin of his face and made him squint. Two of the senior casters were working the bellows in long alternating strokes, and the metal in the pit was the color of old sunsets, orange shading toward white at the center. The noise was enormous. Hammers somewhere behind the partition wall, the bellows breathing their long slow breath, the creak of the overhead chain rig that the casters used to move the molds.

And under all of it, the under-sound.

Cass stopped walking when he heard it. He'd been hearing it all his life, or not-hearing it, the way you didn't hear your own heartbeat until something made you notice. But now he was listening to it differently, the way Maret's question had made him listen differently, and it was like putting your ear to a wall and hearing the house breathe.

It was lower here, near the furnace. Not quieter. Lower in pitch. Like the heat was pulling it down.

He filed that and went to find Edric.

Edric turned before Cass reached him, which he always did, some trick of awareness that Cass had never figured out. He had the look he got when he was thinking about something else — eyes focused on a middle distance that wasn't the foundry floor.

"How'd it go," he said. Not a question exactly.

"She gave me a book." Cass held it up.

Edric looked at the book. Something moved across his face, quick, gone before Cass could name it. "Which one."

"She didn't say the title. It's got a green cover. The pages are—" He stopped, because he'd been about to say the pages are soft, which was true but strange, and he wasn't sure he wanted to explain that he'd kept touching the pages on the walk back. "She said read the first section and come back in three days."

"Right." Edric uncrossed his arms and put his hands in his pockets. He was looking at the book still. "Right, that's — yes."

"You know it."

"I've seen it."

Cass waited. One of the casters said something to the other over the noise of the bellows and they both adjusted their rhythm, the strokes lengthening. The furnace responded with a sound like a large animal settling.

"She ask you anything?" Edric said.

"She asked me to describe the under-sound."

"What'd you say?"

Cass thought about how to put it. He'd been clear enough in Maret's room, or he'd thought he had, but saying it again felt like trying to carry water in your hands — you could do it but you lost some with every step. "I said it was one thing. But that it sounded different depending on where you were standing. Like — the bell doesn't change, but the relationship changes."

Edric was quiet for long enough that one of the casters glanced over at them, checked whatever he needed to check, and looked away again.

"She tell you if that was right?" Edric said.

"She said it was the right question."

Another silence. Then Edric made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh — more like a breath that had decided at the last moment to have some humor in it. "Yes. That sounds like her."

"What's in the book?"

"Read it and find out."

Cass had expected that. He'd asked anyway because sometimes Edric surprised him. He tucked the book back under his arm and looked at the furnace, at the metal in the pit that was too bright to look at directly. The heat was doing something to the air above it, making it move in ways that didn't match the drafts from the bellows. Like the air was trying to sort itself by temperature and not quite managing.

"The under-sound is lower here," he said. "Near the furnace."

Edric looked at him.

"Than it is in the archive passage. It's lower in pitch."

"You're sure."

"I think so." He wasn't entirely sure. He'd only noticed it just now, and noticing something once wasn't the same as knowing it. But it felt true in the way that things felt true before you had language for them, before you could check the feeling against anything. "I'd want to listen in more places. To be sure."

Edric looked back at the furnace. "Don't do it today. Your head'll be tired from Maret. Give it a day."

Cass's head was tired from Maret. He hadn't noticed until Edric said it, but there was a thickness behind his eyes, not the needle but something duller, the feeling of having held a sustained concentration for too long. Like the muscles that moved his eyes had been doing extra work.

"Tomorrow?" he said.

"Day after." Edric pulled one hand out of his pocket and made a small gesture that meant they were done talking about this. "You eat?"

"Not since noon."

"Kitchen's still open. Tell Sera I said."

He went.

---

The kitchen passage was narrow and smelled of rendered fat and the dried herbs that hung in bundles from the ceiling — rosemary, thyme, something bitter he'd never learned the name of. He had to turn sideways to get past the barrel that someone had left blocking half the corridor, the book pressed flat against his chest so it wouldn't catch on the stone wall.

Sera was banking the big fire when he came in, pushing coals to the sides of the grate with a long-handled iron tool. She was a broad woman, somewhere past fifty, with forearms like a caster's and a general air of having seen most things and found them unsurprising.

"Edric says," Cass started.

"Sit down." She didn't look at him. "There's bread and the end of the bean pot. The bean pot's better than it sounds."

He sat at the long table and set the book face-down in front of him. He didn't want to open it in here, not with grease in the air and the risk of Sera asking what it was. He just wanted to know it was there.

Sera brought the bread and the pot and a cup of small beer and set them down and then stood looking at him with her arms crossed, which was her way of saying she was going to ask something.

"You were up in the old rooms," she said.

"Archive passage."

"The old rooms at the end." She said it flatly. Not a question.

"Yes."

She looked at him for another moment. Her expression was the one she used for things she'd decided not to say. Then she went back to the fire and he ate.

The bread was two days old and the bean pot was in fact better than it sounded — someone had put smoked pork in it, and a bay leaf, and the beans had gone soft and rich and deeply themselves. He ate more than he'd meant to. The small beer was thin but cold, and the cold was good after the foundry floor.

He kept his hand near the book while he ate. Not on it. Near it.

When he was done Sera came and took the pot without asking if he was finished, which meant she'd been watching him and knew he was. She stood at the washing basin with her back to him.

"She's not unkind," Sera said. "In case you were wondering."

Cass hadn't been wondering that exactly. He'd been thinking about the room, the smell of it, the lamp that had made everything amber. "I know."

"People say things." Sera set the pot in the basin and picked up the bread plate. "She's been here a long time. People say things about people who've been here a long time."

"What do they say?"

Sera turned and looked at him, and her expression had changed to the one that meant she'd said more than she'd meant to. "Never you mind. Go on, then. You've got reading."

He took the book and went.

His room was on the upper floor of the east wing, a small room with a window that looked out over the secondary courtyard — not the main casting yard but the smaller one with the cooling racks and the finished bells waiting for collection. On a still night you could hear the bells shift in the wind, not ringing but something smaller, a breath of metal. He'd slept to that sound his whole life.

He lit the lamp on the table by the window and sat and opened the book.

The first page had no title. Just a date, written in a hand that had gone spidery with age, and below the date a single line:

*These are not instructions. Do not read them as instructions.*

He turned the page.

The first section was twelve pages. He read it twice, because the first time through he understood the words but not what they were doing together, the way you could understand every note in a sequence and still not hear the tune. The second time was better.

It was about listening. Not the act of it, not technique, but what listening was — what happened in the space between a sound and a person's awareness of it. The writer kept using a word Cass didn't know, a word from a language he didn't have, and had written a long parenthetical trying to explain it: *(the word means something like the moment before recognition, when the sound has entered the body but the mind has not yet decided what it is. There is no good word for this in the common tongue. I have tried several and none of them fit.)*

Cass read that parenthetical three times.

He sat back and looked at the ceiling, which was low and had a water stain in the corner shaped like nothing in particular. The lamp made the stain shift slightly, the edges of it moving as the flame moved.

The moment before recognition. When the sound has entered the body.

He put his hand flat on the table. He could feel the grain of the wood under his palm, the slight roughness of it, the warmth the lamp had put into it. He couldn't feel the under-sound. He'd never felt it as a physical thing, only heard it, or not-heard it, and the needle behind his eye when it spiked.

But the book was saying — or he thought it was saying, he wasn't sure he was reading it right — that hearing and feeling weren't as separate as they seemed. That the body received sound before the mind claimed it. That there was a window, a very brief window, in which the sound was in you but not yet named.

He thought about the furnace floor. The under-sound lower in pitch near the heat.

He thought about Maret's room, the amber light, her hands around the cup.

He thought about the needle, and how it always came from the left, and how it had been quiet in Maret's room even though the under-sound was there. He'd been listening hard. He'd been paying attention. The needle had rested.

He opened the book again and read the section a third time, more slowly, and this time he stopped at every sentence that confused him and sat with the confusion instead of moving past it.

By the time he finished the lamp had burned down by a third and the courtyard outside was dark. He could hear the bells on the cooling rack. Wind from the north tonight, small and cold, and the bells answering it with that breath of metal, not music exactly but something that had music's grammar.

He closed the book and put it on the table beside the lamp and looked at it.

The cover was green. In the lamplight it was very dark green, almost black at the edges. He'd been touching the pages all evening and his fingertips felt the texture of them still, that strange softness.

He should sleep. He knew he should sleep. Edric had said his head would be tired and it was, the dull weight behind his eyes that had been there since the foundry floor. But there was something else too, something that wasn't tiredness — a feeling like standing at the edge of something and not yet knowing if it was a cliff or a step down.

He got up and went to the window.

The courtyard below was lit by one torch at the far gate, and in its light he could see the cooling racks, the rows of bells in their wooden cradles. Twelve of them tonight, or he thought twelve — it was hard to count in the torchlight. They were in various sizes, from the large one at the end that was waiting for the abbey at Correth to the small run of sanctary bells along the near wall, and the wind was moving through them all with the same cold breath, and each one answered it differently.

Same wind. Different bells.

He stood at the window and listened until the torch at the gate guttered and he couldn't see the bells anymore, only hear them.

The under-sound was there. It was always there. But he was thinking about what the book had said — the moment before recognition, when the sound has entered the body but the mind has not yet decided what it is — and he tried to find that moment. Tried to catch himself before he named it. It was like trying to watch himself fall asleep. Every time he almost had it, the having-of-it woke him up.

He went to bed.

He lay in the dark and listened to the bells and the wind and under all of it the thing that was neither, and the needle behind his left eye was quiet, resting, and he thought: she said it was the right question. She didn't say it was the right answer.

Then he thought: the writer of the book didn't know the word for it in the common tongue. But they knew there was a word. They knew the thing existed. They wrote the book anyway.

Sleep came sideways, the way it did when he was working on something, not a decision but a slow failure of the thing keeping him awake.

---

He was back in Maret's room in the morning, before he meant to be.

Not physically. He was at the long table in the archive, where Edric had set him to cataloguing the commission records from the last three years, a task that involved reading a great deal of bad handwriting and writing a moderate amount of his own. But his mind kept pulling back to the room. The amber light. The smell of mineral and old wax. The cup between her hands.

She'd asked him to describe the under-sound and he'd said it was one thing that sounded different depending on where you stood.

She'd said: go read the first section.

He'd read the first section three times and he was fairly sure he understood what it was saying but he was not sure he understood what it had to do with what he'd said, and that gap was the thing his mind kept going back to, the way your tongue found a loose tooth.

He wrote a commission number in the wrong column, noticed, scratched it out, wrote it again.

The book was upstairs in his room. He'd left it there deliberately because he'd known he'd be doing the catalogue work today and he'd known he'd want to read it instead, and leaving it in his room was the closest he could get to not having it with him.

He heard Edric's footstep in the corridor before Edric appeared, because Edric had a particular way of walking in the archive passage — slightly slower than his normal pace, like the passage itself required a different speed. He appeared in the doorway and looked at the table and at Cass and at the commission records spread across the table.

"How far are you."

"Two months back." Cass gestured at the stack to his right. "Another two hours, maybe."

Edric came in and sat down across from him. He didn't often do that — he usually leaned in the doorway or stood. Sitting down meant he wanted to talk about something.

Cass kept writing. He'd learned that with Edric, sometimes the thing to do was keep doing the thing you were doing.

"The book," Edric said. "You read it."

"Three times."

Edric looked at his hands on the table. He had a caster's hands — broad, scarred across the knuckles, the skin permanently darkened in the creases from metal and fire. "And."

"And I'm not sure I understand what it's got to do with what I told her."

"What do you think it's got to do with it."

Cass put down his pen. This was one of Edric's habits that he sometimes found useful and sometimes found maddening, the answering of questions with questions. Today it felt like being handed a tool he didn't know how to use. "I don't know. That's what I just said."

"What did the first section say."

"It said—" He stopped. He'd been about to summarize it, but summarizing it felt wrong, like pouring the bean pot into a different vessel and calling it the same thing. "It said there's a moment between when a sound enters the body and when the mind names it. And that most people never find that moment because the naming happens too fast."

Edric was quiet.

"And I think it's saying the under-sound lives there," Cass said. "In that moment. I think that's what it's saying. That the reason most people can't hear it — or can't hear it as a thing, as one thing — is because their minds name it wrong before they can get there." He picked up the pen again, turned it over in his fingers. "But I don't know what that has to do with me saying it was one thing that sounded different in different places. I don't see the connection."

Edric said, "You told her it was one thing before you read the book."

Cass looked at him.

"You already knew it was one thing," Edric said. "You didn't have to read the section to know that. You knew it because of how you hear it. The book is explaining why you can hear it that way when most people can't. The connection you're looking for — that is the connection."

Cass sat with that.

The archive smelled of dust and tallow and old paper. From somewhere on the foundry floor a hammer was working a steady rhythm against what sounded like a cracked mold, checking for the fault line. He knew that sound — had heard it a thousand times — the way a careful tap traveled through bad metal differently than good.

"She knew that," he said. "Before I said anything. She already knew I could hear it."

"Yes."

"So the question wasn't to find out what I knew. It was to find out how I knew it."

Edric picked up one of the commission records from the stack, looked at it without reading it, set it down. "You should finish the catalogue."

"Edric."

"You should finish the catalogue and then eat something and then tomorrow you can walk the foundry floor and listen to the under-sound in different places if you want. But today you finish the catalogue."

It was not unkind. It was just the way Edric ended conversations that had gotten to the place he'd meant to get them, and Cass recognized it. He picked up the pen.

Edric stood and went to the door. He stopped there, which he sometimes did, and Cass waited.

"She asked me the same question," Edric said. "When I was about your age. I told her it was many things, all different." A pause. "She gave me a different book."

He left.

Cass looked at the doorway for a moment, then at the commission records, then at the pen in his hand.

Many things, all different. Edric had heard it as many things. And Maret had given him a different book, which meant a different starting place, which meant — he didn't know exactly what it meant, but the shape of it was becoming clearer, the way shapes became clear in low light if you stopped trying to look directly at them.

He went back to the catalogue. He wrote carefully. The hammer on the foundry floor found the fault line eventually — the sound of it changed, the tap becoming a slightly different tap, and then stopping — and he tracked it without meaning to, the way you tracked weather without deciding to.

The under-sound was there, low and constant, doing what it always did.

He let it sit in the moment before he named it, just for a breath.

It felt different that way. Not bigger or smaller. Just — present in a way it hadn't been before, the way a person in a room became more present when you finally looked at them instead of around them.

He wrote another commission number. He got it in the right column this time.
