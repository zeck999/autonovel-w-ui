# Chapter Two

Morning came in through the gap under the dormitory door as a line of grey, and Cass was already awake.

He had been awake since the fourth hour, maybe before. At some point the under-note had faded, or he had stopped being able to separate it from the general silence of the building, which was not truly silence at all but the sound of eleven boys breathing in various states of distress. Tomm's wet rattle. Pell's even draw and release. Someone near the window, one of the Harwick twins, who made a small clicking sound on every exhale that Cass had learned to sleep through in his first week and had apparently unlearned sometime in the night.

He got up before the bell. The apprentices' bell, he meant, the one mounted on the post outside the washroom that Edric struck at half-six with a short iron rod. Not the other one.

The floor was cold through his socks. He found his trousers in the dark by feel, his shirt, the heavy wool overshirt he'd bought with his own money at the autumn market because the dormitory was drafty and the House did not supply winter clothing beyond a single blanket. He dressed without lighting his candle. He was good in the dark. His father had always said that — *you're a creature of the dark, Cass, you'd navigate a cave by sound alone* — and Cass had taken it as the mild insult it was probably not meant to be.

He went out.

---

The courtyard between the dormitory and the foundry was still in shadow, the sun not yet above the eastern roofline. His breath fogged. The cobblestones were slick with last night's damp and he picked his way across them carefully, not from fear of falling but from a habit of quietness he'd had since childhood, a preference for moving through space without announcing himself.

The foundry door was unlocked. It was always unlocked; Edric believed in access to tools, or said he did, and the truest expression of this was that he left the door open and trusted that any apprentice who came in early was there to work and not to steal. Three boys had stolen in Cass's time here. Two had been sent home. The third, a boy named Seve who'd taken a finished bell-cup and pawned it across the river, had simply vanished one morning and no one had explained where.

Inside, the forge fires were banked but still warm. The room held the heat from the previous day the way old stone held summer — reluctantly, in pockets, giving it up slowly. Cass stood just inside the door and let his eyes adjust. The familiar shapes came up out of the dark: the long casting table, the row of molds on their shelves, the chains hanging from the ceiling beam, the tool rack with its silhouettes of hammers and tongs and swage blocks and the two good mandrels that Edric kept wrapped in oilcloth when they weren't in use.

He went to the casting table.

Yesterday's work was still there — a set of temple bells, small ones, the kind that hung in doorways and rang when the wind moved them. They were for a commission Edric had mentioned only in passing, some minor religious house in the Terrath district that wanted twelve matched bells in three sizes. Cass had helped with the mold-making, had mixed the investment compound, had done the pour on two of the smaller ones under Edric's supervision. The bells were out of their molds now, rough still, the sprues not yet cut, the surfaces carrying the texture of the sand in faint stippled patterns.

He picked up the smallest one. Held it by the sprue, which was still attached, a thumb-thick column of bronze that would be cut off and melted back down. He turned it slowly.

The bell was not yet a bell. It was a bell-shaped object, which was different. The shaping was done, the casting done, but the voice wasn't in it yet. The voice came from the finishing — from the tuning, from the careful removal of metal at specific points along the rim and the shoulder, from the fitting of the clapper to the right weight and the right hang. You could take two bells cast from the same mold and tune them to different notes, or you could take a bell that looked right and find it was cracked somewhere inside, a flaw in the cooling, and no amount of finishing would make it sing.

Cass's father would have said something here about character. Cass set the bell down before the thought could complete itself.

He found the finishing tools where they always were, selected a scraper and a small half-round file, and pulled a stool up to the table. If he had two hours before Edric's bell, he could get through the roughing-out on three of the small ones. It wasn't assigned work. Edric hadn't told him to do it. But Edric would see that it was done and would know who had done it and would say nothing, which was the closest Edric came to approval.

He set the bell in the leather cradle, took up the scraper, and began.

The work was quiet and specific. The scraper moved in short strokes along the outer surface, raising thin curls of bronze that fell onto the table and caught the banked light. Cass worked by feel as much as sight, pressing just hard enough, reading the resistance in his hand, learning the particular character of this bell's metal. Every pour was slightly different. The alloy was the same — ten parts copper, one part tin, Edric's proportion, which he'd gotten from his own master and would not vary — but the character of the metal changed with the temperature of the pour, the humidity of the day, a hundred small variables that added up to something you could only learn with your hands.

His left eye had started aching again. Low, specific, just behind the socket. He ignored it.

He had gotten through the roughing on the first bell and was halfway through the second when he heard footsteps outside and the door opened and it was not Edric.

It was a girl. Fourteen, maybe fifteen, wearing an apprentice's apron over a dress that had been good once and was now very clean but worn at the cuffs. Her hair was dark and cut short in a way that suggested practicality rather than fashion. She stopped when she saw him, assessed him for a moment with the particular look of someone recalculating, and then came in anyway and went directly to the far end of the casting table where a set of larger molds was stacked.

Cass watched her. She did not look at him.

"I haven't seen you before," he said, which was true and also the least interesting thing about the situation.

"I started three weeks ago." She lifted one of the molds, checked the seam, set it down. "You're Cass."

"Yes."

"Edric talks about you."

He waited for her to say more. She didn't. She moved to the next mold.

"What does he say?"

"That you have good ears and bad instincts." She said it the way you'd report weather. "He means it as a compliment. I think."

Cass put the scraper down. "What's your name?"

"Maret." She looked up then, briefly, and he saw that she had the kind of face that was all angles at rest and probably looked entirely different when she was pleased about something, though he had no evidence of that yet. "I'm not apprenticed to Edric. I'm apprenticed to Foundress Yule. She lends me out sometimes for heavy work."

"Foundress Yule." He knew the name. A senior member of the Guild, one of the few women who'd made master in the last generation. He'd never seen her. "What's the heavy work?"

"Today? Moving those." She gestured at the molds. "We're setting up for the large commission."

"The Terrath bells."

"The Terrath bells are small. I mean the other one." She paused, seemed to reconsider something, and went back to checking the seam on the mold. "Never mind. It's not my commission to talk about."

He picked up the scraper again. Worked for a moment. The eye ached.

"Maret."

"What."

"Do you hear anything? Right now."

She went still. It was a specific kind of stillness, the kind that meant she was actually listening rather than waiting for him to finish. He noted that.

"The forge," she said. "The river, far off. Your scraper." She paused. "What am I supposed to be hearing?"

"Probably nothing." He pressed two fingers against his left temple. The ache was a point, not a spread — a needle-tip of pressure that came and went with his pulse. "I heard something last night. Low. Below everything else."

"A bell."

He looked at her. "Pell said the same thing."

"Pell Harwick?"

"Yes."

"Pell Harwick has the worst ears in this building. He once failed to hear Edric shout his name from six feet away." She said this without contempt, just accuracy. "If Pell heard it too, it wasn't subtle."

"It wasn't subtle." He set the scraper down again. He seemed to keep doing that. "It was coming up through the ground. Or — it felt like that. Like the building was resonating with something underneath."

Maret had stopped pretending to check the molds. She was looking at him now, both hands flat on the table. Her expression had not changed, exactly, but something in it had shifted, the way a bell's note shifts when you move your hand near the rim.

"You should talk to Foundress Yule," she said.

"I don't know Foundress Yule."

"I know. I'm saying you should." She picked up the mold again, decisive, done with the conversation. "She'll be in the east annex after the morning bell. If you want."

"Why would she want to talk to me?"

Maret looked at him over the mold with an expression he couldn't quite read — not pity, not quite — and said, "Because you're the second one this month to hear it."

---

Edric's bell rang. The dormitory came to life in the particular way of eleven boys who all wanted to be last out of bed: a series of sounds suggesting motion without actual motion, and then, because the cold always won, actual motion. Cass heard them through the foundry wall, heard the washroom door bang open, heard Pell's voice raised in complaint about the temperature of the water.

He stayed at the casting table. He had finished three bells. They sat in the leather cradle in a row, their surfaces smooth now, ready for the tuning. He looked at them without seeing them.

*The second one this month.*

Who was the first?

He thought of going to find Maret and asking, but she had taken her molds and gone out through the side door into the annex passageway ten minutes ago, moving quickly, as if she'd already said more than she'd meant to.

Edric came in at half-past six exactly, as he always did, wearing his leather apron over his morning clothes and carrying a tin mug of something that steamed. He looked at the three finished bells. He looked at Cass. He drank from his mug.

"Rough-finished," he said.

"Yes."

"Seam on the middle one is uneven. You'll need to go back over the shoulder with the round file before you tune it."

"I saw that. I was going to."

Edric set his mug on the corner of the table and picked up the middle bell, turning it slowly in his hands. His hands were remarkable — Cass had thought so since his first week — wide and scarred and capable of enormous delicacy, the hands of someone who had spent forty years in conversation with metal. He ran his thumb along the seam line where Cass had missed the irregularity.

"Not bad for dark work," Edric said. "You were here before light."

"I couldn't sleep."

Edric set the bell down. He had the quality of seeming to hear more than was said, or perhaps of simply waiting long enough that the speaker filled the silence themselves. Cass had learned to wait him out.

"The sound again," Edric said, finally.

"Yes."

"Show me where it hurts."

Cass touched two fingers to his left temple, then moved them slightly, pressing against the socket just below the brow. "Here. It's not — it's not bad. Just specific."

Edric studied him. "Behind the eye, or at the surface?"

"Behind."

"Left only?"

"Yes."

"And the sound itself. Same character as last night?"

"Lower, I think. Or I was closer to it. Lying down, maybe that made a difference." He heard himself trying to be precise, the way Edric had trained him, and pushed further. "It had a fundamental. I couldn't identify the pitch, it was too low, but it had — harmonics above it. The way a big bell does. You feel the fundamental in your chest and you hear the partials with your ears."

Edric was quiet for a moment. The morning light was coming through the high windows now, proper light, and it caught the side of his face and showed his age in a way the dimmer hours didn't. He was not a young man. He had been master of the House of Bells for twenty-two years and had learned his craft from a woman who had learned it from a man who had learned it before the Guild was the Guild, when bell-making was still counted among the sacred trades and the foundry workers had worn white.

"Edric," Cass said. "Maret told me I'm not the first."

A pause. "She shouldn't have told you that."

"Who was the first?"

"An adult." He said it as if that settled something. "Someone with more context for what they were hearing."

"What were they hearing?"

Edric picked up his mug again. He looked at the bells on the table, at the window, at the door. He was a man who made decisions through a process that was entirely interior and emerged only as finished conclusions.

"Come to my study after the noon break," he said. "Bring the middle bell. I want to hear you tune it."

"That's not an answer."

"No." He moved toward the forge. "It's an appointment."

The other apprentices began arriving then, in ones and twos, sleep-creased and unhappy about it, and the morning's work began the way it always did, with Edric assigning tasks in a voice that did not invite response and the boys distributing themselves through the foundry like water finding its level. Pell ended up at the bellows. Tomm, whose color this morning was a shade Cass associated with impending vomiting, was set to mold-cleaning, which was the lightest work. The Harwick twins — Dav and Seve, no relation to the vanished Seve, just an unlucky coincidence of names — were assigned to the crucible prep.

Cass was told to continue with the temple bells.

He worked. The needle in his eye came and went, sharper when he bent his head forward, easier when he straightened. By mid-morning he had tuned two of the small bells to a note he was reasonably confident about and had set the third aside because something in it rang wrong — a faint shadow behind the main note, a second voice where there should be one, the signature of an internal crack or an inclusion in the metal. He set it apart from the others and put a chip of charcoal on the table beside it so Edric would see it had been flagged.

At the noon break the boys went to the refectory. Cass took the middle bell, the one with the uneven seam, and went to Edric's study.

The study was a small room off the main foundry floor, more storage than study, with shelves of ledgers and reference books and a worktable buried under papers and samples and the kind of accumulated objects that collect around a man who has been doing one thing for a very long time. There was a bell on every surface. Small ones, mostly, demonstration pieces and experiments and failures kept for the information they contained. One very old one on the highest shelf, black with age, that Cass had never seen Edric touch.

Edric was already there, sitting behind the worktable, reading something. He did not look up when Cass came in.

Cass sat in the single chair across from him and set the bell on the edge of the table and waited.

"The Foundress will see you tomorrow morning," Edric said, without looking up. "Early. Before the work bell."

Cass thought of Maret's expression. The shift in it. "You spoke to her this morning."

"I sent a note." He turned a page. "She's been expecting you."

"She doesn't know me."

"She knows of you." He looked up then, and his face had the quality it sometimes had of a man who had arrived at a decision and was now simply reporting it. "Your father wrote to her when he wrote to me. She and your father have a — they knew each other. Years ago."

Cass looked at the bell on the table. It was easier to look at the bell.

"He wrote to a lot of people," Cass said.

"He wrote to the right people." Edric folded his hands on the papers. "I want you to understand something. The sound you're hearing — the thing beneath the city — it's not a symptom of something wrong with you. Or not only that. Do you understand the difference?"

"No."

"Good. Honest answer." He stood, moved around the table, picked up the bell. "Let me hear you tune it. The rest we'll discuss tomorrow."

Cass took the bell and the half-round file and began. He worked the shoulder first, where the seam irregularity had thrown off the curve, taking metal in short careful strokes and stopping to strike the bell and listen each time. The note was flat. He worked it up, degree by degree. Edric stood behind him and said nothing.

The needle in his eye pulsed once, hard, when he struck the bell on the sixth trial, and the note rang out clear and he felt the harmonics above it in the bones of his hand, and below that, faint and terrible and unmistakably there, the other sound answered.

Not from the bell.

From below.

He stopped. Held the bell still. The note faded. The under-sound faded with it, or retreated, or simply waited.

"You heard it," Edric said. He was not asking.

"When I struck the bell."

"Yes."

"It — answered. Or it was already there and the bell made it audible."

"Which do you think?"

Cass set the bell down on the worktable and pressed his fingers hard against his left eye socket. The needle was a spike now, bright and specific, already fading but leaving an afterimage of pain the way a bright light left color on the inside of closed eyelids.

"It was already there," he said. "The bell just gave me something to compare it to."

Edric said nothing for a long moment. Outside, the refectory bell rang the end of the noon break. Boots on the courtyard cobbles, voices, the foundry door swinging open. The day reasserting itself.

"Tomorrow," Edric said. "East annex. I'll take you to her myself."

He picked up the bell from the table and turned it in his hands, running his thumb along the shoulder. "The seam is right. The note is right. This one's finished." He set it with the completed bells on the shelf by the door.

Cass stood and his legs were steadier than he'd expected them to be. The needle behind his eye had gone to a dull pressure, bearable, the kind of thing you learned to carry.

He went back to the foundry floor and found his stool and his tools and the remaining bells, and he worked through the afternoon without speaking much, and when Pell asked him at supper whether he'd figured out the sound he said no, which was not entirely a lie.

He'd figured out part of it. The part that said it was real. The part that said it knew when he was listening.

The rest was for tomorrow, and he found, setting down his spoon and looking at the refectory ceiling with its dark beams and its one good lamp, that he was neither afraid nor relieved but something in between those things, something without a name, the feeling of a bell just before you strike it — full of sound, holding it, not yet rung.
