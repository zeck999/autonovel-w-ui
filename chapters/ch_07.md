# Chapter Seven

The address Maret had given him was on Tanner's Approach, which was not on the approach to anything — it dead-ended at a yard wall where someone had stacked broken crates and left them long enough that moss had colonized the gaps. Cass walked the length of it twice before he found the door, which was set back from the street and had no number on it, only a smear of ochre paint above the lintel that might once have been a symbol.

He knocked.

A long pause. From somewhere inside, the sound of something being moved across a floor — wood on stone, deliberate, not hurried.

Then Maret opened the door.

She was in her shirtsleeves. No coat, which made her look different, less assembled. She had a smear of ink on her left wrist that she'd made some attempt to rub off.

"You're early," she said.

"You said come the day before."

"It's the day before. I meant afternoon." She stepped back from the door anyway.

The room was small and occupied almost entirely by a table covered in papers and a narrow bed pushed against the far wall with a pack on it, half-unpacked. It smelled of ink and the particular close smell of a room where someone had been working for hours with the window shut. A single candle on the table, though there was still enough light coming through the one small window. She'd been here long enough to make the room look like her, and she'd only arrived three days ago. Cass wasn't sure what that meant about her.

She sat back down at the table and picked up a quill and then put it down again, which seemed to be her version of giving him her attention.

"Sit," she said, and nodded at the bed because there was nowhere else.

He sat on the edge of it. The pack shifted and something inside it clinked — glass, or metal.

"I want to ask you something," he said.

"You want to ask me several things. Ask the one you actually came to ask."

He looked at his hands. He'd washed them twice before coming and there was still bronze dust in the creases of his knuckles, the fine reddish grit that lived there no matter what. "What happens to people who have this and don't — do anything with it. Who just go on."

Maret was quiet for a moment. She picked up the quill again, not to write, just to hold. "Define go on."

"Work. Live. My father's a caster. I've been in the yard since I was small. I know how to do things. Useful things." He stopped. "I'm not — I'm not saying I want to stay. I'm asking what happens if someone did."

"To the ability?"

"Yes."

She set the quill down with more care than it needed. "It doesn't go away. It's structural, like — you know how a bell that's been badly tuned will go on being badly tuned? It's in the metal. You can't just decide not to hear it." She looked at him. "You'd have the headaches. More of them, probably, and worse, because you'd be around bells and not knowing what you were responding to. You'd think you were ill. Some people do think that. They find out eventually, usually when something goes wrong."

"What kind of wrong?"

"The kind where the bell they were nearest to when it happened gets examined afterward, and someone finds what they should have found before it was hung." She said it flatly, without drama. A fact. "It's not common. But it happens."

He turned that over. The under-sound was there now, faint, the way it was when he was away from the yard — a baseline, like the sound of his own breathing that he only noticed when he thought about it. "Is that why you came to find me? Because of what might go wrong?"

"I came because Orvast sent me."

"That's not what I asked."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Partly," she said, which was more of an answer than he'd expected.

He looked at the papers on the table. He couldn't read most of them from where he sat but he could see they were covered in diagrams — bell profiles, cross-sections, the kind of technical drawings his father kept in a locked cabinet and consulted when he thought no one was watching. Maret's were annotated in a cramped hand, with measurements and what looked like musical notation running along the margins.

"Can I ask you something else."

"You're going to."

"How old were you when you went to the Hall."

She picked up the quill. "Twelve."

"Did you want to go?"

A pause. She wrote something, a single word at the edge of one of the diagrams. "I wanted to understand what was happening to me. The Hall was where the understanding was." She looked up. "That's different from wanting to go."

He thought about his mother, who had not been home when he left that morning, who had been at her sister's since the day after he'd come back from the pour, which she'd done before when things were difficult and which he understood was not about him specifically and still felt like a door being quietly closed. His father had made breakfast, which he never did, and had put too much salt in the porridge and had not mentioned it, and they had eaten it together in a silence that was almost comfortable.

"My father knows," Cass said. "He hasn't said so, but he knows."

"What does he know?"

"That something's changed." He pressed his knuckles against his knee. "He keeps looking at me like he's checking a measurement."

Maret set the quill down again. Something in her manner shifted — not softer, exactly, but less braced. "He's a caster."

"Yes."

"Has he ever had anything like this?"

"No. I don't think so. He's — he hears bells the way anyone does. He's good at it, but it's not—" He stopped, because he didn't have the language yet for the difference, and trying to find it made the under-sound pulse once, a brief brightening.

The needle arrived. Not the worst it had been — a quick press behind his left eye, like a thumb pushing against the socket from the inside, and then fading.

He pressed two fingers to his temple.

Maret watched him. "How long has that been happening?"

"Since the pour. Longer, maybe. There was a bell last year, a crack we couldn't find. I had — I thought it was headaches from the smoke."

"Was it a crack?"

"Yes. We found it after it was hung. Had to bring it back down." He lowered his hand. The pain was gone, leaving only the awareness of where it had been. "My father was furious. He thought someone had been careless with the cooling."

"Had they?"

"No. The crack was already there. In the original pour, probably." He looked up. "I knew it was wrong. I didn't know I knew."

Maret was quiet. She turned the quill over in her fingers, once. "That's a long time to be carrying something without a name for it."

He didn't answer, because she wasn't wrong and he didn't want to agree out loud.

"The Hall will test you tomorrow," she said, returning to something more solid, something with edges. "It won't be like what you're expecting."

"I don't know what I'm expecting."

"Good. Most people who come in expecting something specific get it wrong and then spend the first month trying to reconcile what they imagined with what's actually there." She pulled one of the papers toward her and turned it so he could see it — a diagram of a bell in cross-section, with lines extending from the rim at various angles. "They'll start with listening. They'll ring bells in a sequence and ask you to describe what you hear. Don't try to use the vocabulary you think they want. Just describe it."

"What vocabulary do they want?"

"Pitch terminology. Overtone series. Proper names for the partials." She tapped the diagram. "You probably know some of it from your father."

"Some."

"Forget it, for the test. If you try to translate what you're actually hearing into the technical terms, you'll lose something in the translation and they'll know." She looked at him directly. "They're not testing whether you've learned the words. They're testing whether the thing is there."

He looked at the diagram. The lines radiating from the bell's rim were labeled in her cramped notation — *hum, prime, tierce, quint, nominal* — and beside each one she'd written something that wasn't a number, a phrase or a fragment. Beside *hum* she'd written *below the floor*. Beside *nominal* she'd written *the one that thinks it's the bell*.

"What does that mean," he said, pointing. "The one that thinks it's the bell."

She looked at where he was pointing. "When you hear a bell ring, what you think you're hearing — the main note, the one that names it — that's the nominal. But it's not the fundamental. The hum is the fundamental, and it's an octave lower, and most people can't hear it at all unless they're trained to listen for it." She paused. "People with the sensitivity hear the hum first. Before the nominal. The bell announces itself from the bottom up."

He sat with that.

The under-sound, right now, in this room, was low. Below where he'd have said sound started. He'd always assumed it was something wrong with his hearing, a ringing, the kind that came from too many hours near the furnace. He'd never thought of it as a note.

"That's what it is," he said. Not a question.

"Probably. We'll know tomorrow."

He stood up, because sitting on the edge of her bed while she watched him understand things felt suddenly too exposed. He moved to the window. The yard wall at the end of Tanner's Approach was visible from here, the stacked crates, the moss. Someone had chalked something on the wall in letters too worn to read.

"Maret."

"Yes."

"If I go. If I do this." He kept his eyes on the wall. "Is there a way to — I mean, do people come back. To their families. Or is it—"

"It's not a cloister." She sounded almost amused, which surprised him. "You'd be an apprentice, not a monk. You'd have leave. The training is three years for the basic qualification, and the Hall is two days' travel from here."

"My father's yard is small. There's my older brother, but he's—" He stopped. Tomás was twenty-two and had been promised to a girl in Velde for a year and was going to go to Velde, which everyone knew and no one said, because saying it would make it real before the family was ready for it to be real. "There are things that need doing."

"There will always be things that need doing."

He turned from the window. She was watching him with an expression that wasn't quite patience — it was more like the look his father got when he was waiting for metal to tell him something. Attentive. Not pushing.

"My father's going to ask me what I want," Cass said. "He's been not-asking it for a week and he's going to ask it tonight, because I'm going to the Hall tomorrow and he knows and I haven't told him and we've been walking around each other in the yard and it's—" He stopped. "I don't know what to tell him."

"Tell him what's true."

"I don't know what's true."

"Tell him that, then."

He looked at her. She said it the way you'd say something obvious, not unkindly, the way you'd tell someone they were holding a tool wrong — here, just do it this way, it's not complicated. He wanted to find something wrong with it and couldn't.

"You make it sound simple."

"It is simple. Not easy. Simple." She picked up the quill again and this time actually wrote something, a few words at the bottom of the diagram, not looking at him. "Your father sounds like someone who can hear the difference."

He thought of the porridge. The too-much salt, the careful silence, his father passing him a piece of bread without being asked because he'd known Cass wouldn't want to ask for it.

"Yes," he said. "He can."

He left shortly after. She didn't walk him to the door — just said *before ninth bell, tomorrow* without looking up from her papers, which he was beginning to understand was its own kind of courtesy, not requiring him to make a proper exit.

The street outside was cooling. The sky above the yard wall was the particular orange-grey of early evening in the city, where the light caught the smoke and turned it almost beautiful. Cass stood for a moment outside her door and listened.

He could hear, from somewhere several streets over, the sound of a bell being rung — not a tower bell, something smaller, a handbell or a shop-bell, a single note struck and then dying. He heard the nominal first, the way he always had, the note that named itself. And then, underneath it, already fading, the thing below it. The hum. The floor of the sound.

It had always been there. He'd just never known it had a name.

He walked back toward the yard.

His father was at the workbench when Cass came in, not at the furnace — it was too late in the day for a pour — but doing the close work, the filing and finishing on a small bell that had come back from a customer with a complaint about the tone. His father did this kind of remediation with a patience he couldn't always find for other things, working in small strokes, checking the pitch after each pass with a mallet tap and a tilt of his head.

He didn't look up when Cass came in.

Cass took his apron from the hook and went to the secondary bench and began sorting the tools that had come back from the morning's work, cleaning the files, checking the handles. It was the kind of task that didn't need doing urgently and both of them knew it.

They worked like that for a while. The small bell rang every few minutes, his father checking, adjusting. It was almost right. Cass could hear it getting closer — the sour edge in the overtone smoothing out, the note clarifying. Almost.

"Who was the woman," his father said. Still not looking up.

"Someone from the Hall of Bells."

His father tapped the bell. Listened. "I thought so."

"You knew?"

"I didn't know. I wondered." He set the mallet down and looked at the bell, turning it slightly on the bench. "There was a man, when I was learning. He came from the Hall to look at a commission we'd done for a temple outside the city. He had a way of standing near a bell before it was rung. Just — standing near it." He picked up the mallet again. "She stood like that. Near the bell you were working on, the morning she came."

Cass set down the file he was holding.

"She says I have something," he said. "A sensitivity. To the — to things in the sound that most people don't hear."

His father tapped the bell once more. It rang out clear, almost perfect, just the faintest shadow of the old complaint still in it. He listened until it died completely.

"I know," he said.

Cass looked at him.

"Not the name for it. I didn't know there was a name." His father put the mallet down and turned on his stool to face him, and his expression was the one Cass had been waiting for all week — not the checking-a-measurement look, something older than that, something that had been there before Cass was old enough to read it. "The crack, last year. You came to me three days before we found it. You said the bell sounded wrong. I asked you how, and you couldn't say. I thought—" He stopped. Rubbed the back of his neck. "I thought you were nervous about the commission."

"I was nervous about the commission."

"Yes, but that wasn't why you came to me."

"No."

His father looked at the small bell on the bench. Then at Cass. "She wants you to go to the Hall."

"They want to test me. Tomorrow." He paused. "She says there's a function. For someone who can find flaws that don't show in the metal."

His father was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Cass heard a cart pass in the street outside, heard the wheels on the stone, felt the under-sound shift and settle.

"That's a real thing," his father said finally. "That function. I've heard of it. I didn't know it was still—" He shook his head. "It's an old position."

"She said that."

"Did she say what it pays?"

The question was so unexpected — so purely his father — that Cass almost laughed. He didn't, but something in his face must have moved, because his father's mouth pulled at one corner.

"It's a reasonable question," his father said.

"She didn't say."

His father nodded slowly. He picked up the small bell and turned it in his hands, not to examine it, just something to hold. "Your brother's going to Velde. We haven't said it, but he is."

"I know."

"This yard is — it's not the same yard it was when I started it. We've lost two commissions this year to Harven's shop because they have a second furnace and can turn things faster." He set the bell down. "I'm telling you this so you know I'm not — I'm not asking you to stay. I want you to know the shape of what's here, so that when you decide, you're deciding about the real thing, not some idea of it."

Cass looked at his father's hands on the bench, the broad knuckles, the permanent staining around the nails. He'd spent fourteen years watching those hands and he still couldn't do what they did, not with that particular certainty, the way his father touched metal and knew things.

"I don't know what I want," Cass said. "I'm going to the test tomorrow because I want to know what's happening to me. After that I don't know."

"That's honest."

"She said to tell you what's true."

His father looked at him. "She sounds sensible."

"She is." He paused. "She's a little frightening."

"Good people to learn from, usually." His father stood up from the stool, and for a moment he just stood there, and Cass thought he was going to say something significant, something that would close the conversation properly, and instead he said: "There's bread from this morning, if you're hungry. And I think there's some of the hard cheese left."

"All right."

"Your mother will be back tomorrow."

"I know."

His father moved toward the door to the house, and then stopped with his hand on the frame. Didn't turn around. "The bell you said was wrong. Last year." A pause. "You were right. I should have listened better."

He went inside.

Cass stood alone in the workshop. The small bell sat on the bench where his father had left it, and the tools were half-sorted in front of him, and the furnace was cold and dark and smelled of ash and old metal. The under-sound was there, steady, neither louder nor softer than it had been all evening.

He picked up the small bell. Held it the way his father had, not to examine it, just to hold it. It was lighter than it looked — they always were, the small ones, the shop bells and the hand bells, the kind that didn't need scaffolding to hang. He could feel the smoothness of the finished bronze, the faint texture where his father had been working, a region near the rim that was microscopically different from the rest.

He turned it slightly.

The under-sound shifted, the way it did when he was near a bell — not louder, but more present, more itself, the way a conversation becomes clearer when you stop thinking about something else and actually listen.

He set the bell down without ringing it.

Tomorrow he would go to Maret's before ninth bell, and then they would go to the Hall together, and someone would ring bells for him in a sequence and ask him to describe what he heard, and he would try to describe it without reaching for the words he thought they wanted. He didn't know what came after that. The not-knowing sat in his chest like a stone, not painfully, just there, present and heavy and real.

He hung up his apron.

The bread was on the table in the kitchen, and the hard cheese beside it, and his father had already gone upstairs, and the house was quiet in the way it was quiet on evenings when nothing was wrong and nothing was settled, and Cass sat down at the table alone and ate, and listened to the house breathe around him, and underneath it, always, the low note that had been there his whole life, waiting for him to learn its name.
