# The Second Son of the House of Bells

## Chapter One

The bronze was wrong.

Cass heard it before he saw it — a flatness in the tone when Master Edric struck the test piece, a sound that should have rung clear and instead died at the edges, swallowed by itself. He was three steps behind the master, carrying the quench bucket, and he stopped walking without meaning to.

Edric struck it again. The same dead thud.

"You heard that," Edric said. Not a question.

"Yes, sir."

The master set down his hammer and turned the piece over in his hands. A bell blank, palm-sized, for a chapel commission out of Westering. It had come out of the mold that morning looking perfect: clean lines, good color, no visible pitting. Cass had thought so himself when he'd helped pull it. He'd been wrong.

"Tell me what's wrong with it," Edric said.

Cass set the bucket down. He took the blank when Edric held it out and turned it the way he'd been taught, feeling the weight distribution, looking for the seam. The metal was still warm from the quench. His thumb found a slight irregularity along the lower third — barely a ridge, almost nothing.

"There's a cold shut here," he said. "The metal folded on itself before it set."

"And?"

He thought about the sound again. The way it had died. "And there's something else. A void somewhere inside. I can't feel it but the tone—" He struck the piece against his palm and listened. "It sounds like it's talking to itself. Like there's a room inside it."

Edric took the blank back. He looked at Cass for a moment with the expression he wore when he was deciding something. Then he set the piece on the reject shelf without another word and walked back toward the furnace.

That was how Cass had spent his mornings for two years: carrying things, listening, being tested. He was fourteen and had come to the foundry at twelve, the second son of a minor house with no land worth speaking of and a name that meant nothing outside the county. His father had apprenticed him to Master Edric because the foundry was respectable and because there was nothing else obvious to do with a second son. His older brother Aldric would inherit the house's three fields and its debts. Cass had been given a trade.

He had not minded. That was the thing he still found strange when he thought about it — he had not minded at all.

---

The foundry sat at the edge of Casterbrook, downhill from the market square, close enough to the river that the apprentices could hear it through the walls when the water ran high. There were four of them: Cass, and Mira who was sixteen and nearly a journeyman, and the Voss brothers, Pell and Tomm, who were cousins despite the name and had come together from somewhere north and spoke to each other in a dialect Cass couldn't follow. They all slept in the loft above the cooling shed. In winter the heat from the day's work rose through the floorboards and kept them warm. In summer it was miserable.

Cass didn't mind that either, mostly.

What he minded was the under-note.

It had started six months ago, or he'd started noticing it six months ago, which wasn't the same thing. A sound beneath sounds — not a sound exactly, more the suggestion of one, a frequency that seemed to exist just past the edge of hearing. He'd thought at first it was tinnitus, the occupational damage that came from working near bells. Edric had warned them all about it. But tinnitus was a ringing, a high whine, and this was lower. Much lower. And it moved.

It was there now, faint, as he swept the casting floor. A pressure more than a pitch, sitting behind his left eye like a needle that hadn't quite broken skin. He stopped sweeping and turned his head, trying to locate it the way you'd locate a sound — by the direction it seemed to come from. North, maybe. Or up. Neither of those made sense.

"You're doing that thing again," Mira said. She was at the workbench, filing a clapper shaft.

"What thing."

"The standing-still-with-your-mouth-open thing."

Cass started sweeping again. "I heard something."

"You always hear something." She wasn't being cruel about it. Mira said most things the way she'd say the temperature of metal: as an observation, with no particular feeling attached. "Edric says you have good ears."

"Edric says I have good ears for the work I've been given."

She glanced up. "That's the same thing."

It wasn't, but he didn't argue. The distinction mattered to him and explaining why would take longer than it was worth. What Edric meant was that Cass could hear defects, could hear the difference between a bell that would ring true and one that would fail — and that was useful, that was what he was for. But the under-note wasn't a defect. It wasn't in the bells. It wasn't in anything he could point to or name. It was somewhere else, and it was getting louder, and he didn't know what that meant.

The needle behind his eye pressed a little deeper. He breathed through it.

"Where's Pell?" he asked.

"Sent to the chandler's. Edric needed beeswax for the lost-mold work."

"Tomm?"

"Sick. Really sick this time, not the kind where he lies in the loft and Pell brings him food."

Cass nodded. He'd heard Tomm coughing in the night, a wet sound that had kept him half-awake. He'd meant to check on him in the morning and then the day had started and he'd forgotten, and now he felt the particular guilt of someone who knows they're capable of forgetting.

He left the broom leaning against the wall and climbed the loft ladder.

Tomm was in his bunk with his coat pulled over him, which told Cass how bad it was — the loft was already hot enough that Cass was sweating just from climbing up. Tomm was sixteen, big-framed, with the kind of face that looked like it had been roughed in quickly and the details forgotten. He was pale in a way that didn't suit him.

"You look terrible," Cass said.

"Thanks." Tomm's voice was a wreck. "Very helpful."

"Do you need water?"

"Had water. Didn't help."

Cass sat on the edge of the nearest trunk. Outside, through the small loft window, he could see the river glinting between the buildings. A cart was crossing the bridge, loaded with something covered in canvas. Ordinary afternoon. "You should tell Edric it's bad."

"Edric'll send me home."

"Maybe you should go home."

Tomm looked at him with the expression of someone who has explained something many times and is tired of explaining it. Cass understood. Going home meant the north, meant whatever Tomm had left behind there, meant the end of the apprenticeship. You didn't get to come back from home.

"Right," Cass said. "Sorry."

"Stop apologizing. You're worse at it than Pell and Pell's terrible at it."

"That's not—" Cass stopped. He'd been about to say *that's not fair*, which was the sort of thing you said when you were twelve. "I'll get you more water anyway."

He climbed back down. The needle was still there, steady, not quite pain. More like the memory of pain, the place in the flesh that remembers the last time something hurt and braces itself.

---

Evening came the way it always did in summer: slowly, with the light going golden and then amber and then finally giving up. Edric sent Mira home early — she lived in town, with her mother — and told Cass to bank the furnace. It was a task that required patience more than skill, the careful arrangement of the damper so the coals would hold through the night without burning too hot or dying completely. Cass had done it enough times that his hands knew the sequence before his mind did.

He was finishing when he heard the bell.

Not from inside. From outside, from somewhere up the hill, toward the square. A single strike, low and resonant, the kind of tone that meant a civic bell, a meeting bell. He counted automatically — four — and then stopped, because there was no civic bell in Casterbrook that rang at this hour for any reason he knew of.

The under-note surged.

He bent forward over the furnace grate, one hand braced against the stone, and the needle behind his eye drove itself in to the hilt. Not a memory of pain now. Actual pain, sharp and specific, the kind that makes the world go white at the edges. He breathed. He counted his own breaths. After four of them the pain stepped back, not gone but manageable, a coal instead of a flame.

He straightened up. His hand was shaking slightly. He looked at it as if it belonged to someone else.

Outside, the bell rang again. Five.

Cass went to the foundry door and opened it. The evening air smelled of the river and of someone's supper fire, bread and fat. The street was quiet. A woman walked past with a basket, not hurrying, not looking at him. No one else seemed to have heard anything unusual. The sound had been real — he was certain of it, the way he was certain of the cold shut in the blank that morning, the same quality of knowing — but it had not made anyone else stop walking.

He stood in the doorway for a long moment.

"Cass."

Edric was behind him, coming from the back of the foundry with his hands wrapped in a cloth. He was fifty, or somewhere near it, with a face that the heat had simplified over the years — all the soft parts burned away, just the bones left, and the eyes. The eyes were brown and very direct.

"The furnace is banked," Cass said.

"I know. I checked." Edric stopped next to him and looked out at the street. "What are you listening for?"

Cass thought about saying *nothing*. "There was a bell. Up the hill, I think. Five strikes."

Edric was quiet for a moment. "The civic bell doesn't ring at evening."

"I know."

"What did it sound like?"

Strange question. Cass thought about it seriously, the way Edric had taught him to think about sounds — not just *loud* or *soft* but the actual quality, the thing the sound was made of. "Old," he said finally. "Like the metal had been poured a long time ago. And—" He stopped.

"And?"

"And it sounded like it was underground. Like it was coming up through the ground instead of through the air."

Edric looked at him with that expression again, the one from this morning. Deciding something. The cloth in his hands moved slowly, the way it did when he was working a problem.

"Have you been sleeping?" Edric asked.

"Yes."

"Headaches?"

The needle, still there, still specific, still behind the left eye. "Sometimes."

Edric nodded. He turned and went back inside without explaining what he'd been deciding, which was his way. Cass stood in the doorway another moment, watching the street. The woman with the basket was gone. The supper smell was fainter. Up the hill, if he looked at the right angle, he could see the corner of the square, the edge of the fountain, the top of the old building that had been a guildhall once and was now something else, a records office or a grain store, he'd never been sure.

He went back inside and climbed up to check on Tomm.

Tomm had kicked off the coat and was asleep, or something close to it, his breathing ragged but regular. Pell still wasn't back from the chandler's. Cass sat on the trunk again and listened to Tomm breathe and tried to locate the under-note by its direction the way you'd locate a sound in a dark room.

Up the hill. That was his best answer. Up the hill and down.

He pulled his knees up and leaned his back against the wall. Through the loft window the sky had gone a deep blue, not quite dark, the last of the light still caught somewhere above the clouds. He'd been in Casterbrook two years and he still didn't know it well — knew the foundry, knew the market, knew the route to the river where they washed the tools. The rest of it was a rough sketch. He'd never been curious enough to fill it in, or maybe he'd been too tired, or maybe he'd been waiting for something without knowing he was waiting.

The under-note settled into a low, even pulse. His eye ached dully around it.

He thought about the bell. The quality of it — old metal, deep pour, the sound coming up through stone. In two years of working the foundry he'd learned that every bell was a record of its making: the alloy, the mold, the temperature, the pour, all of it written in the tone. A bell told you what it was made of if you knew how to listen. And the bell up the hill had told him something he didn't have a word for yet, something about age and depth and intention, as if whoever had cast it had been trying to reach someone specific.

Pell's boots hit the ladder rungs. He came up with the beeswax wrapped in paper, set it on his trunk, looked at Tomm, looked at Cass.

"How is he?"

"Sleeping," Cass said. "Still hot."

Pell sat down heavily. He was fifteen and built like someone had tried to make a smaller version of Tomm and run out of material partway through. "Edric's going to send him home."

"Maybe."

"He will." Pell unwrapped the beeswax and wrapped it again, just to have something to do with his hands. "Then it'll just be us three."

Cass thought about that. Three apprentices and a master for a commission list that had grown since Edric's reputation had spread south — the Westering chapel, a set of processional bells for some lord's house, a clock mechanism with four quarters. Too much work. Edric would need to take on someone new.

"Pell," he said. "Have you heard anything strange lately? A bell, maybe, up near the square?"

Pell gave him the look that meant he thought Cass was being odd. "A bell."

"Forget it."

"There's no bell up near the square. The civic bell is on the east side, by the—"

"I know where the civic bell is."

Pell rewrapped the beeswax a third time and left it alone. Tomm made a sound in his sleep, not a word, just a sound, the kind that meant the body was working on something. After a while Pell climbed into his own bunk and pulled his blanket up and was asleep in what seemed like moments. That was a gift Cass had never had — the ability to simply stop and go under.

He lay on his own bunk and stared at the ceiling and listened.

The under-note was still there. Steady, patient, low. Like a bell that had been struck an hour ago and hadn't finished ringing yet. Like something waiting for him to answer.

His eye ached. He pressed two fingers against the socket, not hard, just enough to feel the shape of the pain. Behind his left eye, specific as a name.

He thought about what he'd said to Edric. *It sounded like it was coming up through the ground.* He'd meant it as description, as an attempt at precision, the way Edric had trained him. But lying in the dark with Pell snoring softly and Tomm's breathing wet and labored, the words came back to him differently. Not description. Diagnosis.

Something beneath the city was ringing.

And it had been ringing, he understood now with the particular certainty of things you've known for a while without letting yourself know them, since the first morning he'd walked through Casterbrook with his father's letter in his coat and his single bag over his shoulder and looked up at the foundry sign — a bell in profile, bronze paint on dark wood — and felt, for no reason he could name, that he had arrived somewhere he was supposed to be.

He lay still. The ceiling was invisible in the dark. Somewhere down the hill the river ran, indifferent, over its stones.

The bell rang once more, very faint, very far below.

Cass closed his eyes and listened until he couldn't tell the sound from his own pulse.
